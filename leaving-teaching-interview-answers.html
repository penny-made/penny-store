Why are you leaving teaching? Interview answers for former teachers (free)

Leaving teaching means explaining your experience to people who have never run a classroom. This page has interview answers for that: “Why are you leaving teaching?”, two more questions worth having ready, which teaching stories fit behavioral questions, and questions to ask them.

Prepare for the questions below. Not every interviewer asks them, but they're worth having ready, in your own words and with your own facts.

“Why are you leaving teaching?”

Shape: one true, forward-looking reason, what draws you to this work, and the link to this role. Keep it short, and don't criticize students, families or colleagues.

I've loved [the part of teaching you're keeping: designing lessons / helping people learn / working with families]. Over the last [time], I've found that the part I'm best at and enjoy most is [that part], and I want to do it [for adults / with a product / full-time]. This role is a chance to do that, especially [one thing from the posting].

If burnout or pay is part of your reason, you don't have to lead with it, and you shouldn't deny it if asked. A calm, true line can cover it: “Teaching asked a lot beyond the teaching itself, and I want to put my energy into [the work this role does].”

“Won't you miss the classroom?”

Some parts, yes, like [a true example]. The part I'm taking with me is [the part this role uses], and here I'd get to do it [more / with adults / at a larger scale].

“You haven't worked outside a school. How will you adjust?”

Shape: what's the same, what's new, and a step you've already taken.

The core of this job is [a duty from the posting], which I've done for [N] years with [students / colleagues / families]. What's new to me is [a real gap: the business side, a tool, the pace]. I've started on it by [a real step: a course, a work sample, talking with people in the role].

Behavioral questions (“Tell me about a time when…”)

Teaching gives you plenty of stories. Pick the ones that look most like the new job:

  • conflict: a disagreement with a colleague or a parent that you resolved
  • data: a time results showed a problem and you changed your approach
  • training: a time you taught adults (colleagues, families, volunteers)
  • project: an event or program you organized against a deadline

Tell each one in the new field's words, make your own part clear (“I”, not “we”), and leave out anything that could identify a student.

Questions to ask them

  • What do people who came from teaching usually find easiest here, and hardest?
  • What would a successful first 90 days look like in this role?
  • How does the team share knowledge or train new people?
